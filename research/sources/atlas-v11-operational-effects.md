# Operational effects in the European air war

Seven studies follow the people who depended on an air operation. Each gives the period, beneficiary, competing explanations and the point where the evidence runs out. The accompanying audit compares both sides in all 20 campaign dossiers with their v10.1 coverage. ## Supplies that reached the troops

**Arnhem, 18–25 September 1944.** The divisional headquarters report distinguishes net supplies from gross loads including containers and parachutes. Its central collections on 19 and 20 September amounted to 21 of 388 and 41 of 386 net tons. The latter records two collections of 3 and 38. The 49 Air Despatch Group diary provides the departure-side record and approximate drop weights. The diary places a lift on 22 September, while the division lists no mission that day and a lift on 23 September. The divisional return attributes eight tons to aircraft from Brussels on 25 September. The RAF Museum describes the aircraft returning without a ground signal. The disagreement remains visible under the date each source gives. [Dispatch diary, WO 166/15581](https://www.pegasusarchive.org/arnhem/war_49adg.htm) · [Divisional headquarters, Part II, appendices D and E(i), WO 171/392–393](https://www.pegasusarchive.org/arnhem/war_divhq.htm) · [RAF Museum, chapter 5](https://www.rafmuseum.org.uk/blog/the-royal-air-force-and-operation-market-garden-chapter-5/)

The division published a 7.4% recovery rate and 106 tons collected. Fourteen tons came from landed Hamilcar gliders. Deducting those gives 92 centrally counted tons from 1,431 dropped, or 6.4%. Units probably collected more directly. Headquarters estimated a further 100 tons and expressly lacked figures to substantiate them. Nearly all stores recovered were eventually issued, according to the same report. These statements come from one headquarters and do not independently corroborate one another. [Divisional report, Part II, appendix E(i), notes 1–3](https://www.pegasusarchive.org/arnhem/war_divhq.htm)

**Stalingrad, November 1942 – January 1943.** The US Army official history distinguishes aircraft that started on 29 November from aircraft committed on 30 November. Sixth Army’s quartermaster recorded 125 tons received on 16 December, about two-thirds fuel, and a pressing need for bread. A later compilation assigns 174 tons flown in to the same day. Treating the difference as undelivered cargo would require an outward manifest, common cargo definition and matched flight records. The German transport assessment provides operational context. It does not settle the disagreement. The priority remains the outbound returns and corresponding army dump and unit-issue records for the same day. [US Army official history, chapter XXIII](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USA/USA-EF-Decision/USA-EF-Decision-23.html) · [Wehner quoting KTB Qum, 16 December, p. 42](https://www.academia.edu/41734605/STALINGRAD_BREAKOUT_AND_AIRLIFT_OPERATION_A_MATTER_OF_CHOICE) · [Transport assessment, RAF AHB translation VII/69](https://www.raf.mod.uk/what-we-do/our-history/air-historical-branch/ahb-translations-from-captured-german-documents/the-use-of-transport-aircraft-in-the-present-war/) · [Daily reconstruction](https://losthistory.net/stalingrad/germanpart/airliftdec.html)

The army field postmaster’s March 1943 report offers a narrower receiving account. Inbound mail reconciles to 89,073 kg (73,173 through December plus 15,900 through 26 January). The two outbound figures add to 22,355 kg, while the transcription prints 32,355. The author also explains why evacuating aircraft could carry uncounted outward mail. The inbound series stands separately from food and fuel. Checking the microfilm would help distinguish a transcription error from a discrepancy in the report. [Ziegler, paras 1–2, T77-R1036, pp. 74–75](https://wehrmacht-auskunftstelle.de/feldpostversorgung-in-stalingrad/)

## What ships and beaches experienced

**Sicily, 10–12 July 1943.** The western naval plot counted 89 raids aimed at the assault areas. It recorded 26 driven off before entry and 63 entering. Some low approaches escaped the plot. A separate account from Captain Harwood of USS Joseph T. Dickman describes unloading continuing through attacks, with a 10–15 minute interruption while the ship shifted berth. Unloading finished on the afternoon of 11 July. The ammunition ship Robert Rowan was lost the same day. No. 92 Squadron’s record identifies patrols in the Pachino sector. Assigning the Gela transport’s experience to one of those patrols would require a matching escort and ship log. [Eighth Fleet administrative history, part IV, section XXXIV](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/Admin-Hist/148.3-Sicily/148.3-Sicily-4.html) · [Harwood interview, pp. 29–33](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USCG/X-Sicily-Italy/USCG-X-1.html) · [Army official history, pp. 177–178](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-D114-PURL-gpo89315/pdf/GOVPUB-D114-PURL-gpo89315.pdf) · [No. 92 Squadron Form 540](https://www.92-squadron.com/copy-of-form-540-oct-1941-sept-1942)

**Normandy, 6–10 June 1944.** The air commander gives the allocation covering the invasion. The Western Naval Task Force reports conditions above the fleet and distinguishes fighter cover from requested direct-support missions. Its 42-minute interruption on 10 June applies to cover. Its figures of 27 requests and 12 missions flown apply to the day’s direct support. Utah was unloading efficiently by D+1 and Omaha by D+2. Enemy ground resistance, beach obstacles and the organization of unloading also differed. On the eastern beaches, Browne’s and Bennett’s accounts give separate pilot and infantry observations. There is no common measure of tonnage saved by a patrol. [CTF 122, air support and build-up annexes](https://ee.valka.cz/doc/pdf/192.pdf) · [AEAF despatch, paras 311–314](https://www.raf.mod.uk/what-we-do/our-history/air-historical-branch/ahb-despatches-and-reports/despatch-by-air-commander-in-chief-aeaf-to-supreme-allied-commander-aef-covering-operations-15nov1943-30sep1944/) · [Canadian recollections](https://canadashistory.ca/explore/military-war/d-day-memories)

## Recovery and replacement in Germany

**Bearings.** At Schweinfurt, local output in September 1943 stood at 35% of the pre-raid level. The survey’s recovery by autumn 1944 describes the bearing industry. The RAF history distinguishes August and October damage to machinery and finished stocks. Inventories, substitution and imports helped users absorb a temporary supply reduction. Neither account provides a continuous monthly series for the individual works. Both histories drew on German industrial evidence. [USSBS, The Ball-Bearing Attack, pp. 5–6](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/AAF/USSBS/ETO-Summary) · [RAF AHB, bombing offensive, vol. V, pp. 143–144](https://www.raf.mod.uk/what-we-do/our-history/air-historical-branch/second-world-war-campaign-narratives/raf-in-the-bombing-offensive-against-germany-vol-v-the-full-offensive-february-1943-to-february-1944/)

**Fuel.** Leuna repeatedly resumed partial output. The site reached 75% of capacity in early July 1944 and averaged 9% between the first attack and the end of the war. National synthetic aviation gasoline production fell from 175,000 tons in April to 5,000 in September. These are different products, populations, periods and units. The survey documents constraints on pilot training. Linking a particular plant shipment with a particular flying unit would require dispatch and distribution records. A further free plant-level source is the 1945 technical investigation of the Leuna works. [USSBS, The Attack on Oil, pp. 8–9](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/AAF/USSBS/ETO-Summary) · [Bureau of Mines, Information Circular 7370](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc67033/)

**Replacement aircraft.** The survey reports acceptances by type in 1943. A separate official-history series puts fighter production in 1944 alongside the strength and serviceability of single-engine fighters on reporting dates. Production rose between February and September despite raids. The serviceability share fell from 70% in March to 59% in June before reaching 71% in September. Dispersal, expanded production, repairs, losses and crew availability complicate the passage from factory output to available combat units. The stock and flow series cannot identify individual replacements. [USSBS, The Attack on German Aircraft Plants, pp. 6–7](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/AAF/USSBS/ETO-Summary) · [The War in the Air 1914–1994, table 2, printed p. 121, after Webster and Frankland](https://media.defense.gov/2017/Apr/07/2001728538/-1/-1/0/B_0078_STEPHENS_WAR_IN_AIR.PDF)

## All 20 dossiers

Each of the 40 assessments names a specific question that the evidence answers, partly answers or leaves open. A linked dated case supplies the source passages. Each side has a next document or comparison to pursue. Eighteen questions have a bounded answer, 19 have partial evidence and three remain open. The categories describe the question chosen for each side. They cannot measure the overall completeness of a campaign.

The same counting rules apply to both editions. The published v10.1 baseline has 297 named memberships in 44 dated lists. The subsequent expansion to 984 memberships in 170 lists came with v10.2. The v11.0 audit retains those counts and adds the judgements. The CSV includes the individual source locators, the seven comparable measures, and text on effort, losses and operational effect.
