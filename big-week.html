Operation Argument · 20–25 February 1944

What did Big Week change?

Attacking aviation factories forced German fighters to contest raids escorted deep into the Reich. Some factories suffered severe damage. Germany could disperse machines and put factories on extra shifts. Replacing the airmen lost in combat posed a different problem.

24–25 February · Germany and Austria

One day, three forces

From England by day

Eighth Air Force Mission 233 sent 809 bombers against several targets and lost 49. Among them the 445th Bomb Group sent 25 aircraft to Gotha and lost 13. The group's loss was part of the mission loss. Photographs showed severe damage to the Gotha plant.

From Italy by day

Of 114 Fifteenth Air Force bombers in the two groups recorded in the official history, 87 flew toward Steyr and 27 struck Fiume after separating. The history attributes 17 losses to the Steyr force. Fiume's refinery belonged to a different target system.

After dark

RAF Bomber Command sent 734 aircraft to Schweinfurt on the night of 24/25 February. Its return lists 635 attacking and 33 missing. Separately, Mediterranean-based RAF No. 205 Group attacked Steyr. After the two raids on Schweinfurt, daylight photographs showed damage at all five ball-bearing plants. RAF analysts could see the damage but could not attribute it to the American daylight raid or the British night raid.

Why nearly 4,000 and about 6,000 both appear

The US Air Force museum counts nearly 4,000 USAAF heavy-bomber sorties over the six days. Air University historian Silvano Wueschner reports about 6,000 Allied sorties for the broader operation. His account gives no complete breakdown by command and role. Subtracting the two figures would not establish an RAF total.

Wueschner reports 357 Allied bombers and 28 escort fighters lost, and 262 German fighters lost. His 250 German airmen killed or wounded include 100 pilots. The museum's figure of more than 200 US heavy bombers lost has a narrower scope. These accounts support a comparison of the costs each describes, without assigning one side's aircraft losses to the other side's fighters.

Factories could recover faster than experienced crews

In Case Studies in the Achievement of Air Superiority, the fighter-production series falls from 1,555 in January to 1,104 in February and rises to 1,638 in March. The study describes dispersal, extra shifts and longer working weeks. A damaged plant could still cost Germany planned output even as national deliveries rose. February's national total covers weeks outside the operation.

The Allied air forces could now bring German pilots into battle far from the coast. The aircraft-production series shows why factory output alone cannot measure the resulting change in German fighter defence. The remaining question is how quickly individual units replaced their experienced crews.
