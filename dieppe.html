Operation Jubilee · 19 August 1942 · English Channel

What did air cover achieve at Dieppe?

Fighters protected the ships carrying the raiding force and covered the boats sent back for the troops. Smoke hid part of their approach. Men on the beaches still faced fire from the cliffs, and German aircraft reached the ships offshore.

Manston to Dieppe · Morning

Cover for the ships

At 06:45 Squadron Leader Ford led No. 403 Squadron RCAF out from Manston. The squadron diary puts the Spitfires over Dieppe at 07:15, flying close cover for the ships. Three aircraft failed to return from this patrol. The RCAF later identified a collision in combat as the cause of two of those losses. No. 403 was one of the 74 Allied squadrons allocated across all roles.

The German Commander-in-Chief West reported that smoke from ships and aircraft obscured the landing craft approaching the coast. From the shore the effect was visible, even where an observer could not distinguish which service had laid the screen.

403 Squadron diary, 19 August, first patrol · RCAF account of the collision · German observations of 23 August, no. 8, §2, translated in Canadian AHQ Report 10

Dieppe harbour and beaches · Late morning to afternoon

Cover for the withdrawal

The commanders moved the intended withdrawal from 10:30 to 11:00. That gave the air force time to strengthen cover and the army time to try to reach the Camerons. Small landing craft went in under fighter cover, naval fire and smoke. The screen helped them approach, but the boats still came under fire close to shore.

No. 403 Squadron left Manston again at 11:15 to cover the ships’ withdrawal. All twelve aircraft on this patrol returned at 13:15. Offshore, a bomb hit HMS Berkeley at about 13:00 and the British sank the crippled destroyer. Inshore, air attacks also scattered some landing craft. Many troops on the main beaches could not be taken off.

On its 16:20 patrol the squadron found most of the ships it observed already more than halfway home. The diary follows the returning fleet. The army and naval accounts explain what had happened to the people still ashore.

403 Squadron diary, second and third patrols · Canadian Army, Six Years of War, pp. 383–385

Who counted the losses?

106 aircraft in the RAF account
The Canadian Army’s 1948 summary assigns 106 aircraft to the RAF effort. This is the population label used for the existing Atlas figure. Canadian Army, chapter V, p. 83
Approximately 106 Allied aircraft
The RCAF retrospective uses a broader label and explicitly calls the number approximate. RCAF, closing assessment
108 Allied aircraft
The French ministry’s account gives 108. A common aircraft-by-aircraft return would be needed to explain the difference. French Ministry of the Armed Forces, raid outcome
48 German aircraft destroyed and 24 damaged
The 1948 Canadian Army summary takes these two categories from captured German records. The French account also gives 48 German aircraft lost. Squadron victory claims are reported by a different side and have a different definition. Canadian Army, German loss assessment
