v2.4 — historical source review and Mach-sensitive propeller research

Read the source evidence first. A 1938 full-scale NACA investigation, TR-639, documents propeller compressibility losses that vary with blade pitch and tip speed. A 1945 P-47D flight study (MR L5G09a) reports about 7% improvement in propeller efficiency when reducing tip Mach from 1.16 to 1.06 at matched J≈2.5 and power coefficient per blade≈0.08. The selectable M scenario combines the existing NACA 1941 chart with a transparent Mach-loss function interpreting the published 7 percent as a relative improvement. This interpretation remains to be confirmed against original paired flight data.

Important: M is not a validated propeller map for P-51B, Bf109F, P-47B, or Spitfire. The onset Mach 0.95 is an explicit assumption; the exact blade-angle/Mach effects cannot be transferred from the three-blade 6507A-2. The 12 individual 1945 P-47D flight rows are from the same test campaign as the ratio used by M, so they are a diagnostic, not an independent holdout for M. M defaults off and C remains the general model default.

Period research changes our priorities

1941 low-speed loading: more solidity helps highly loaded props, but four blades do not guarantee a uniform η improvement at all speeds. 1944 cooling duct: full-scale XP-47 duct pressure recovery changed with propeller slipstream, climb attitude, inlet velocity, radiator resistance and exit flaps—not a fixed drag bonus. 1939 heat-recovery trial: no practical recovered thrust up to 400 mph in the tested ducts. 1943 altitude cooling: later calculations explicitly balance cooling pumping, duct drag and heat-jet recovery, including the practical-versus-ideal installation distinction. 1943 XP-51 / 1945 P-47D: actual wing profile drag and transition depend on Reynolds number, lift coefficient and wing condition. 1941 engine altitude methods: power-versus-altitude is supercharger/cooling/ram-specific, not simply ambient density times rated power.

Reference archive and method: separate report registry, full scans, original figure pages, extracted text, pages and SHA-256 hashes, source-chain follow-ups, numerical v2.3 benchmark, and 24+ automated tests. Primary originals are from NASA's NACA collection. IL-2/DCS remain *secondary* and variant-specific. No new drag/power/low-speed coefficient has been tuned to the Mustang's historical climb or the IL-2 turn figures.

v2.1 / Predictive installed engine power at actual flight speed

New opt-in physics: pressure available at the air inlet changes with TAS; a boost regulator holds the selected manifold pressure whenever the supercharger has enough capacity. At each historically identified maximum-speed full-throttle height, the limit is set by the aircraft's recorded speed and engine boost. The engine model predicts boost at a different airspeed without reading the climb MAP.

What is computed?

p₀ = p · (1 + 0.2 M²)^3.5 for subsonic inlet stagnation pressure, ideal pressure recovery assumption. Below full-throttle limit, the regulator keeps MAP at its rating. Above the limit, available MAP falls according to the source critical-pressure ratio. At the same altitude, the solver compares MAP at actual TAS to MAP at reported maximum-level TAS and applies a provisional constant-RPM BHP ∝ absolute MAP ratio. No new aircraft-specific low-speed drag or propeller coefficient is fitted.

Important limitation: “100% recovered” is the best-case ideal intake, NOT a measured efficiency. A separate 70% sensitivity curve is shown. We still lack installed induction-system pressure and measured propeller blade-pitch/η maps. Input critical points are source specific and Bf 109 has only secondary-source critical speed.

A source-backed warning about our common propeller heuristic

NACA TN 2022 (1950) directly measured three-bladed propellers on other radial fighters. The F-4 additionally shows an unfitted emergency-rating check, which comes from the same published Rechlin-based synthesis as its combat anchor.

P-47B and P-51B flight-test tables are HTML transcriptions of wartime reports, not original page-by-page scan audits. P-51B power is Packard chart-derived. Sources are linked under each aircraft's details.

10 / What is known versus assumed?

Scientific limitations and next validation work

Critical: The model fits CD₀ using one reported maximum-speed anchor for each aircraft. This exactly constrains one equation but cannot independently determine both propulsive efficiency and parasite drag. The assumed Oswald parameter determines induced drag and sustained turns. The maximum-lift coefficients are provisional and therefore the stall boundary and turn curves are illustrative.

Bf 109: secondary approximate engine horsepower at 0–15,748 ft only; its sea-level 325 mph is not a matched primary test specimen, and Greg's screenshot does not provide a validated individual power-versus-altitude curve. Above this range no calculated comparison is offered.

P-47: Greg's high-power P-47 spreadsheet and Greg's high-altitude P-47B graph are separate datasets. Do not transfer 2,210 propulsive hp to the P-47B. The P-47B sourced speed tests are with cooling flaps flush, while climb testing opens shutters; no configuration-specific cooling drag yet.

Stall / airspeed: P-47 manual 115 mph IAS and P-51B flight-test 86 mph IAS are not calibrated EAS. We do not reverse-engineer exact CLmax from uncorrected IAS. CAS and TAS conversions are subsonic ISA theoretical; true IAS needs equipment/position calibration.

Propeller: Candidate A/B/C models are exploratory. Candidate C has not reproduced the six-propeller controlled P-47B experiment; no claim of NACA-validated blade efficiency is made.
