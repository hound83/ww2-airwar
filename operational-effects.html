How much supply reached the troops?

At Arnhem, dispatchers and the receiving division kept separate daily accounts. At Stalingrad, the army’s cargo receipts and the postmaster’s surviving returns cover different goods. Compare the journeys through the records each recipient kept.

November 1942 – January 1943

Stalingrad: getting in and feeding the pocket

German Sixth Army, its dumps, and the soldiers they supplied.

On 29 November 25 of the 59 aircraft that took off landed in the pocket. The next day 66 landed out of 77 committed. A later receipt shows why cargo composition mattered. On 16 December the quartermaster recorded 125 tons, roughly two-thirds fuel, and called for bread.

Weather, the loss of landing grounds, the changing size of the pocket and combat all affected deliveries. Army priorities also changed. A load that met a fuel request did little for the ration issue.

What the records leave open

No matched weighed departure manifest and unit issue return was located for 16 December. A secondary daily reconstruction lists 174 tons flown in that day. Both are arrival descriptions with different provenance. The discrepancy cannot measure cargo lost en route.

Campaign evidence · Stalingrad

18–25 September 1944

Arnhem: from the aircraft to the supply dump

British 1st Airborne Division around Oosterbeek.

On 19 September the division counted 21 net tons gathered from a drop it put at 388. On 20 September it gathered 41 from 386. The two headquarters broadly agree on the size of those drops, then diverge on the later lifts.

The perimeter contracted and the division lost collection vehicles. By the last day it counted two serviceable jeeps and trailers. Supplies could land in British lines and be used before the divisional dump saw them.

What the records leave open

The division published 106 tons collected and 7.4% for the operation. The 106 includes 14 from landed gliders on 18 September. The 1,431-ton total in appendix D covers drops. The further 100 tons suggested by the division was an unmeasured estimate. The dates and scopes do not support a single independently verified collection rate. Removing the glider cargo leaves 92 recorded tons from drops, or 6.4% of the division’s 1,431. This narrower calculation still excludes unrecorded local collections.

Campaign evidence · Market Garden

What could the landing forces do under air cover?

Sicily’s western task force counted raids stopped before reaching the ships. In Normandy the fleet described little daylight opposition, then recorded very different unloading conditions on its two beaches.

10–12 July 1943

Sicily: ships could work even when attacks got through

Troops landing in Sicily and the transports unloading in the western assault areas.

The Western naval plot distinguishes raids driven off from raids that entered the assault areas. At Gela, Captain Harwood reported that Joseph T. Dickman could keep unloading through attacks. Near misses forced a short shift of anchorage. The ship finished unloading on the afternoon of 11 July. The ammunition ship Robert Rowan was lost the same day.

Harwood credited his crew and sent men ashore when the Army shore party was diverted to defend the beach. Shore fighting and unloading arrangements also governed the pace.

What the records leave open

The naval plot omits low raids it could not track. No. 92 Squadron’s record of patrols over Pachino documents a different sector. Neither account supplies an estimate of ships that would have been lost without cover.

Campaign evidence · Sicily

6–10 June 1944

Normandy: cover overhead, different results on the beaches

Shipping and landing forces off Utah and Omaha, with a separate Canadian view from Juno.

The Western Naval Task Force recorded its fighter cover in place on D-Day and slight enemy activity after dark. Utah was unloading efficiently by 7 June and Omaha by 8 June. On the eastern beaches, a Canadian pilot remembered four patrols while an infantryman coming ashore at Juno described taking machine-gun fire.

Under the same western air cover, Omaha faced obstacles, stranded craft and fire at the exits. At Utah unloading was already proceeding on schedule. On 10 June weather interrupted afternoon cover for 42 minutes.

What the records leave open

The naval commander credited the deterrent effect of the fighters. The reports establish that ships worked and describe the threats they faced. They cannot count attacks averted or assign an extra ton of supplies to a particular patrol.

Campaign evidence · Normandy

How long did industrial disruption last?

Recovery took different forms. Bearing stocks cushioned factory losses. Synthetic fuel remained scarce. Fighter output recovered while operational strength and serviceability followed their own course.

August 1943 – autumn 1944

Schweinfurt: a factory loss that stocks could absorb

German bearing producers and manufacturers using their output.

Schweinfurt’s September output fell to 35% of its pre-raid level. Production recovered to the pre-raid level by autumn 1944 in the survey’s wider industry account. Its investigators could establish no measurable loss of essential finished war production attributable to the bearing shortage.

Users drew down stocks, changed components and obtained bearings from other sources. Many machine tools survived damage to buildings.

What the records leave open

The September percentage describes the local centre. The later recovery statement concerns the industry. The records do not supply a continuous local series between those two points.

Campaign evidence · Schweinfurt

April 1944 – spring 1945

German fuel: partial reopening and a prolonged shortfall

German aviation fuel users and the people rebuilding the synthetic plants.

Leuna returned to partial operation about ten days after its May shutdown. Its output later reached three-quarters of capacity, then fell again. At the national level, synthetic aviation gasoline output in September was under 3% of April’s. The survey connects the shortage to further limits on flight training.

Repeated disruption and repair changed Leuna’s monthly results. The loss of Romanian crude in August also affected the broader fuel economy. Crude imports and synthetic aviation gasoline belong to different series.

What the records leave open

Leuna’s plant utilisation measures all its production against plant capacity. National aviation gasoline output measures tons produced. The surviving series does not trace a particular batch of fuel to a flying unit.

Campaign evidence · Defence of the Reich

July 1943 – December 1944

Germany: replacement aircraft and aircraft fit to fly

Aircraft factories, Luftwaffe acceptance officers and the fighter force.

Bf 109 acceptances fell from 725 in July 1943 to 357 in December. In 1944 the published fighter-production series rose from 1,555 in January to 3,375 in September, despite the February offensive. At the September strength return, 71% of the single-engine fighter stock was serviceable.

Factory dispersal interrupted work, then spare capacity, surviving machinery and the fighter programme supported recovery. Bad weather delayed acceptance flights. Fuel, crews and maintenance affected what units could use.

What the records leave open

Monthly production is a flow; strength is a stock and serviceability a share of that stock. No linked factory-to-squadron receipt ledger was found for these national figures. A higher output cannot identify losses replaced at a particular unit.
